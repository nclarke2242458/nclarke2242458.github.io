**Is Twine the new close reading?**

As infant scholars, we MAs in the UVA English department have had many conversations about generative AI in the First-Year Writing classroom. We’ve covered its effects on student reading, attention spans, in-class versus out-of-class essays, and the importance of teaching critical analysis. Some of us have started to say we've exhausted the topic, at least on the student side.

I don’t think it is. We can talk about generative AI in ways that go beyond threats and warnings. Ysabel Yates’s recent New Yorker piece, [“Cookie Monster Tries to Meet His A.I. Mandate,”](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/10/05/cookie-monster-tries-to-meet-his-ai-mandate) is one example. The question I keep coming back to is what we lose in uniqueness and personality when we hand our work to AI. Students need to feel they have direction and a voice in a world that is being automated for efficiency, a world that leaves many of them feeling behind. If we look like we’re trying to stop the clock, they’ll tune us out. So how do we help them get comfortable with technology while also helping them trust what they bring that technology can’t replace, especially now that AI is reaching creative work in the humanities?

Professors Anastasia Salter and Emily Johnson’s workshop based on their book, [“Critical Making in the Age of AI,”](https://services.publishing.umich.edu/Books/C/Critical-Making-in-the-Age-of-AI3) offered one answer: Twine.

[Twine’s website](https://twinery.org/) describes it as a free, open-source tool for telling interactive, nonlinear stories. You don’t need to code to make a simple story, but you can add variables, conditional logic, images, CSS, and JavaScript as you get comfortable. Stories publish as HTML, and anything you make is yours to use however you like. 

Salter and Johnson use Twine for creative storytelling in some of their classes. I want to use it for analysis, building on [Mike Bunn’s “How to Read Like a Writer.](https://pressbooks.cuny.edu/qcenglish130writingguides/chapter/read-like-a-writer-by-mike-bunn/)” I teach ENWR 1510, “Writing About Monster,” which treats writing as critical inquiry by engaging with others’ ideas, thinking on the page, and reflecting on forms of writing. I introduce analysis of writing through Bunn’s essay and its central claim that “all writing consists of a series of choices.” For Bunn, reading like a writer means finding the most important of those choices, from overall structure down to a single word, and asking how they affect readers. Then you go one step further and attempt to imagine what the author might have done instead, and what that would change for the reader. This approach works well for nonfiction and academic writing, but fiction is understandably harder for my first-year students. Students often don’t know where to start with a fiction author’s choices, and the process can feel muddy and nerve-racking.

How could Twine change that for them? It would certainly take multiple days. First, students would reconstruct the text as the author wrote it. Mapping the story’s path would slow their reading and force them to annotate and list events as they occur. Then, students would revisit each event or choice they recognized and branch off at those points into choices the author didn’t make. This would require a lot of creativity. Perhaps the story would take a completely different direction, or perhaps the student might find a point where events loop back to the original ending. Either way, the explicit choices become clearer. Students get their hands dirty in the literal nitty-gritty of the fiction, characters, and the butterfly effects of every minor detail included.

Bunn borrows Allen Tate’s image of reading as an architect rather than a historian, studying a building “down to the last nail” so you can build one yourself. Twine provides a platform that helps students map the text they can write about in their analysis. 

AI gives students the comfort of feeling safe about their answers. A machine trained on the work of millions has helped them (or just handed them the answer). When students develop invisible alternatives in a piece of writing, it gives them the freedom to explore the author's mind visually. It also makes them confront the concept of the audience, but that’s a conversation for another blog post.
